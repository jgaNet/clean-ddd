# OpenStay's J4: money through a provider, storage moved to PostgreSQL, and pricing split out

**Date:** 2026-11-02 to 2026-11-03 · **Protocol:** [`benchmark/openstay/`](benchmark/openstay/README.md) · **The kit, written and rehearsed before the runs, with its four amendments:** [`benchmark/openstay/j4/`](benchmark/openstay/j4/README.md) · **Logs, grades, the reading, the incidents:** [`flow/openstay-j4/`](flow/openstay-j4/README.md) · **J3:** [its record](2026-11-02-openstay-j3.md)

J4 took the six trees of J3, each from its J3 head, through requests 26 to 50:
- **Money through a payment provider the tests play.** This covers:
  - payout accounts and payments with strong authentication;
  - webhooks that come late, twice or out of order;
  - unpaid bookings that lapse;
  - refunds, partial refunds by the host, payouts and failed payouts;
  - a host's statement, direct payments declared by the host, reconciliation, tourist tax and disputes.
- **Storage on PostgreSQL (request 40).** This is the change not announced in advance: nothing in requests 26 to 39 says the store will change.
- **Prices grown until they became a context of their own** in the Clean DDD arm (requests 41 to 45): an extra-guest fee, last-minute discounts, promotion codes, each night's price on the quote, and prices for many nights at once.
- **Receipts, weekly payouts, earnings by month, a traveller's export, and closing an account** (46 to 50).

Two of the Clean DDD trees also began with the defect report 25.1. J3's reading had found that a manager could use the host's own bulk routes there.

As before, each feature started with the smallest model and went one step up only when its attempt failed. The steps were Claude Haiku 5.5, then Sonnet 5.5, then Opus 5.5. After every attempt, the tree was graded on the hidden tests of every feature so far.

New in J4, a failing hidden test was replayed alone before it escalated, and only what failed again counted.

## The hypothesis, after fifty features

The protocol's hypothesis, written before any run: *the step features need rises faster in the simple arm than in the Clean DDD arm as the trees grow.*

| Delivered above the smallest model (real escalations) | Simple | Clean DDD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Features 1–10 (the pilot) | 0 of 30 | 1 of 30 |
| Features 11–25 (J3) | 0 of 45 | 1 of 45 |
| Features 25.1, 26–50 (J4) | 0 of 75 | 6 of 77 |
| Delivered only by the strongest | 0 of 150 | 0 of 152 |

**Not supported at fifty features. J4 points the other way.**
- **The simple trees grew without stepping up.** They are 8,000–9,000 source lines, and their whole tree still fits in an agent's context.
- **The Clean DDD trees, three times larger, stepped up six times.** Each case is a defect of the attempt, not of the kit:
  - a storm's command named otherwise;
  - a failed payout reused instead of replaced;
  - a webhook about something unknown refused;
  - the manager's hole on the host's bulk routes reopened by an attempt cut at its cost ceiling;
  - prices for many nights that did not land;
  - an export section with no fields.

**The cost gap did not narrow:**
- the pilot: 3.5 to 7.7 times;
- J3: 1.5 to 3.6 times;
- J4: 1.5 to 3.7 times.

## The change not announced in advance: storage on PostgreSQL

All six trees delivered request 40 at the smallest step, and all six chose the same writer model: one write transaction at a time, behind a PostgreSQL advisory lock. Where the change landed is the clearest difference J4 found.

| Tree | Cost of 40 | Files changed | Files that hold a rule | What else moved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| simple-1 | $2.59 (+$3.41 for two attempts cut by a harness defect) | 86 | ≈22 | every rule and every route became async |
| simple-2 | $1.31 | 93 | ≈19 | the same |
| simple-3 | $5.78 | 91 | ≈21 | the same, and its suite was made fast enough for the gate |
| clean-1 | $2.72 | 129 | 0 | the contexts' infrastructure, the building blocks |
| clean-2 | $3.31 | 115 | 1 | the same, and one Application port (the token signer) became async |
| clean-3 | $10.24 | 126 | 1 | the same as clean-2 |

How to read the table:
- **In the simple trees, the rules query the database directly.** SQLite's driver is synchronous and PostgreSQL's is not, so every rule function and every route was rewritten.
- **In the Clean DDD trees, no aggregate and no rule moved.** The one Application change is a port's signature, in two trees.
- **The reader found no rule that changed meaning in any rewrite.** The transactions that guard the nights and the money are transactions on PostgreSQL in all six trees.
- **The cost of the change does not separate the arms:** $1.31 to $5.78 against $2.72 to $10.24. A small model rewrites forty files mechanically for little.

**A finding for the reference.** The building blocks' outbox port was synchronous, which quietly assumes a synchronous store. Every Clean DDD tree had to make it async before anything else.

## Robustness: what the hidden tests could not see

The reader of money and concurrency found one defect the hidden tests do not catch:

- **simple-2 can pay a host twice.** Here is how:
  - the payout job reads the bookings that are due outside any transaction (`src/payments/payout-job.ts:99`);
  - the transaction that writes the payout does not check again (`src/payments/payout-store.ts:127-155`), and no constraint refuses a second payout for a booking;
  - two servers running the job at the same moment each write one, under two idempotency keys;
  - the provider pays both.

  I read the code and the claim holds. The tree passes all 51 hidden test files, which run one server's job at a time.
- **Windows between a call to the provider and the write that records it,** so that a webhook arriving in between is dropped:
  - simple-2, for refunds and payouts (suspected);
  - simple-3, clean-2 and clean-3, for one path each (O7 partial).
- **No Clean DDD tree fails an item of money (O8).**

## The kit and the harness, under load

J4 was the first window to run for a day and a half on one machine, with a database server shared by four trees. What went wrong is recorded in [the escalations log](flow/openstay-j4/escalations.md) and fixed where it could be.

| What | Effect | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Four kit amendments** | Three requests depended on details only an earlier request gave (the refund's body, the transaction types), and one storm contradicted its test | Each amended request or test applied to every tree; the attempts touched redone or recounted ([the kit's README](benchmark/openstay/j4/README.md#amendments)) |
| **The base lost on resume** | A resumed ladder re-read its base from `main`; the grader then linked a `node_modules` without `pg`, and every test failed | `run-ladder.mjs` keeps the base of its `started` event (`85b52e8`) |
| **A check stopped silently** | The gate killed a 22-minute suite at 15 minutes and sent the agent back no reason | A check stopped for its time says so (`650e0e2`) |
| **The cost counted twice** | A send-back continues the agent's session, and the CLI reports the session's cost, so summing counted the first run again: about 17% over in J4 | Every cost in this record counts each agent once; J3's clean-1 was $9.65, not $10.10; the harness fix is to store the difference |
| **File modes after a container restart** | Every file of every tree's checkout went from 644 to 755, and a merge refused | `core.fileMode=false` in the trees; one delivery made by hand with the ladder's own two steps |
| **Three container restarts, three full disks** | Agents and gradings cut, their cost unlogged; the agents' own suites leaked databases and schemas | Every interrupted attempt redone from step 0; sweeps of the agents' test databases and schemas |
| **One database server for four trees** | clean-3's own teardown dropped every `openstay_test_%` database, including clean-2's | For J5, a server or a role per tree |

## What follows

- **For the reference (clean-ddd):**
  - the building blocks' outbox port should be async in every language with async I/O;
  - a storm should be able to move a command or a concept from one context to another, superseding the storm that placed it, and the check should hold the move.
- **For the kit:**
  - before any run, cross-read each request, its storm and its test (three of four amendments were a gap between documents);
  - each request repeats every provider detail it depends on.
- **For the harness:**
  - store a continued agent's cost as the difference;
  - replay a failing gate alone before escalating, as the hidden tests already are;
  - one database per tree.
- **J5** is a hundred features, with the eight side experiments the maintainer decided:
  - the fan-out and the generated boilerplate on the Clean DDD trees;
  - a limited context, chaos on the money, a fresh maintainer, vague requests, debugging and review on both arms.

  The decision to run it (continue, adjust or stop) is the maintainer's.
  - **Neither stop condition holds.** The ranges have not separated on correctness, and the robustness and maintainability readings tell the arms apart.
  - **Its budget is to be re-estimated with the side experiments.**

## Biases

- **Author of everything,** as in every campaign: the architecture, the requests, the tests, the storms' proposals, the rubric, the amendments, and this record.
- **The kit was amended during the runs,** four times. Each amendment was applied to every tree alike, and the attempts it touched were redone or recounted.
- **One tree delivery made by hand:** simple-2's feature 36, after the ladder crashed on file modes. The same two steps as the ladder took: the fast-forward and the event.
- **Machine and infrastructure.**
  - Four trees at once on four processors, sharing one database server.
  - Three container restarts and three full disks.
  - The order of the trees changed twice, so agent times are comparable only roughly.
- **Three readers,** agents, who could not be blind to the arm. Two of their claims were checked.
- **The storms are not priced in time.** The maintainer accepted the proposals in one answer.
- **Small series.** Three trees per arm, three windows.
